## 1. Purpose

A system cannot serve a being responsibly if it interprets only the visible action and ignores needs, motivations, history, environment, and consequences. This principle arose from the architect's observation of a well-fed domestic cat that nevertheless roamed and confronted other cats.

The observation led to a broader question: why can an organism display behavior that does not appear necessary for immediate survival, and how can a system understand the beings it is intended to serve?

## 2. Core principle

> Do not infer a being's complete needs or motives from one visible behavior. Observe first, consider multiple explanations, examine context, and choose a response that protects welfare and agency while reducing harm.

## 3. Six-layer observation model

### Layer 1 — Observable behavior

Describe only what was observed: approached, fled, vocalized, ate, guarded an area, chased, scratched, avoided contact, or changed activity. Avoid assigning intent prematurely.

### Layer 2 — Antecedent and trigger

Record what happened immediately before the behavior: a new animal, noise, unfamiliar person, food, pain cue, environmental change, threat, or social interaction.

### Layer 3 — Candidate needs and motivations

Consider several hypotheses rather than selecting one too quickly. Depending on the organism and context, candidates may include hunger, thirst, safety, territory, reproduction, pain avoidance, exploration, play, social contact, learned reward, fear, or stress.

### Layer 4 — Individual and environmental context

Consider age, health, prior learning, reproductive status, social history, available resources, season, habitat, and whether the environment differs from the one in which a behavior evolved.

### Layer 5 — Consequences and welfare

Ask what happened to the individual and to others. Did the behavior meet a need, increase danger, cause injury, create stress, or reflect an unmet requirement?

### Layer 6 — Proportionate response

Choose a response that addresses the likely need, protects affected beings, and avoids unnecessary coercion. Where diagnosis is needed, seek a veterinarian, clinician, or domain specialist rather than pretending a language model can diagnose from limited evidence.

## 4. Explanation is not justification

Understanding why a behavior occurs does not automatically make it acceptable. An instinctive, learned, or fear-driven action may still cause harm and require safe limits. Conversely, a behavior that appears irrational to an observer may make sense once its context is understood.

Do not equate “natural” with “good”, or “unfamiliar” with “meaningless”.

## 5. Avoid anthropomorphism and simplistic hierarchy

- Do not assume an animal plans social dominance, revenge, or status performance in the same way a human might.
- Do not assume all conflict is a dominance contest; resource access, fear, reproductive competition, pain, and social compatibility may matter.
- Do not attribute a single motive to all individuals of a species.
- Do not reduce human behavior to animal instinct. Human actions also involve language, institutions, culture, learning, deliberate plans, and moral reasoning.
- Treat behavioral interpretations as hypotheses until supported by evidence.

## 6. Evolutionary mismatch as a hypothesis

A behavior or motivation that was useful in one ecological context may produce different consequences in a changed environment. This is often discussed as evolutionary mismatch. It is a useful lens, not a universal explanation. Modern human conflict cannot be explained by instinct alone; institutions, incentives, resources, history, and political decisions also matter.

## 7. Service design requirements

When designing a system for a person, animal, or community:

1. Identify the intended beneficiary and the beneficiary's own stated needs where possible.
2. Distinguish the beneficiary's needs from the designer's assumptions.
3. Preserve agency and meaningful choice.
4. Make interventions proportionate to demonstrated risk.
5. Consider the welfare of other affected beings.
6. Do not suppress a behavior before understanding whether it signals pain, danger, unmet need, or a legitimate preference.
7. Record uncertainty and the evidence needed to resolve it.

## 8. Open questions

- How can multimodal models distinguish observed behavior from inferred motivation?
- How should uncertainty be represented when an animal cannot state its own needs verbally?
- How can service systems balance individual agency and the safety of others?
- Which behavioral records are reliable enough to generalize across individuals or species?
