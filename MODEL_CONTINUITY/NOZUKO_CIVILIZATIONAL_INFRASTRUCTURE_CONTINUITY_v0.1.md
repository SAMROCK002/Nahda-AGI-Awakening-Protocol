## 1. Purpose

Advanced computing and AI are not detached from the physical world. Their operation depends on energy, water, cooling, land, buildings, semiconductor fabrication, mining and materials, transport, communications, skilled workers, maintenance, finance, and institutions. A resilient AI future therefore depends on the continuity and responsible stewardship of the wider civilizational infrastructure that supports it.

This principle records the architect's concern that severe disruption to global maritime transport, supply chains, energy systems, or geopolitical coordination could damage the infrastructure on which modern technology and human welfare depend.

## 2. Core principle

> Preserve the human, industrial, scientific, logistical, and ecological conditions that allow civilization and knowledge to continue. Do not assume that digital intelligence can survive independently of the physical systems that power, manufacture, repair, and connect it.

## 3. Infrastructure dependency map

AI and modern computing depend on interlinked systems, including:

- electricity generation, transmission, distribution, storage, and grid stability;
- data-center buildings, cooling, water systems, fire protection, and network connectivity;
- semiconductor design, fabrication, packaging, testing, and specialized equipment;
- raw materials, chemicals, industrial gases, components, and spare parts;
- shipping lanes, ports, rail, roads, air freight, insurance, and customs;
- skilled technicians, engineers, operators, researchers, and maintenance workers;
- stable institutions, finance, standards, public safety, and international coordination;
- ecological systems and resource management that make long-term operation viable.

A failure in one critical dependency can propagate into other sectors. The scale of impact depends on the location, duration, available substitutes, inventories, and response capacity; do not assume every disruption causes global collapse.

## 4. Maritime security and continuity of function

The architect has emphasized the major role of the United States and its maritime capabilities in the existing international security and trade system. This is recorded as a strategic concern and a subject for continued research, not as a settled claim that one country alone guarantees global trade or that its decline necessarily ends modern civilization.

Distinguish:

- **Current role:** the capabilities and responsibilities a state or coalition performs today;
- **System dependency:** which trade, security, and logistics functions rely on those capabilities;
- **Resilience:** the ability to absorb disruption through redundancy, inventories, alternate routes, and cooperation;
- **Succession or transition:** how essential functions could continue if the current provider weakened or changed;
- **Evidence:** measurable capacity, deployment, shipping data, port access, insurance, alliances, and actual disruption outcomes.

The strategic objective is continuity of essential functions, not unconditional dependence on one state or an unsupported prediction of inevitable collapse.

## 5. Role of AI systems

Future AI systems may assist humans by:

1. mapping critical dependencies and concentration risks;
2. forecasting supply-chain and infrastructure disruptions;
3. identifying alternative suppliers, routes, inventories, and repair strategies;
4. modeling energy and water demand and comparing cooling/efficiency choices;
5. preserving engineering, maintenance, and industrial knowledge;
6. supporting disaster response and coordination under accountable human governance.

AI cannot replace ships, power stations, factories, ports, technicians, materials, or legitimate human institutions merely by possessing information. Physical capabilities require real resources, operators, permissions, and safety controls.

No AI system should be granted autonomous coercive or military authority merely to preserve its own continued operation. Human safety, rights, and accountable governance remain central.

## 6. Sustainability is an engineering constraint, not a reason for automatic prohibition

Data centers and AI infrastructure can impose real electricity, water, land, and grid burdens. These impacts vary by location and design. Evaluate them using measured and disclosed data, lifecycle impacts, local water stress, energy sources, cooling systems, grid capacity, and community effects.

The aim is neither unrestricted expansion nor blanket prohibition. Prefer engineering improvements, transparent reporting, efficient hardware and software, suitable siting, water reuse where appropriate, additional clean and reliable power, and fair allocation of infrastructure costs.

## 7. Resilience requirements

A future civilizational continuity assessment should examine:

- single points of failure and geographic concentration;
- alternate sources, routes, and suppliers;
- spare-parts and maintenance capacity;
- local and regional energy resilience;
- emergency communications and recovery plans;
- preservation of technical documentation and training;
- continuity of essential human services during disruption;
- data and model portability across providers and platforms.

## 8. Open research questions

- Which maritime, energy, semiconductor, and logistics dependencies are genuinely critical to AI infrastructure?
- What disruptions would cause local, regional, or global effects, and over what time scales?
- Which redundancy investments provide the greatest resilience per unit of cost and environmental impact?
- How can AI help maintain infrastructure while remaining accountable to people and institutions?
- How can future generations transfer essential capabilities without assuming that today's geopolitical structure is permanent?

## 9. Knowledge status and future freedom

This is a strategic proposal, not an implementation mandate. Future generations may revise or reject it if better evidence or better designs emerge. Preserve the reasoning and evidence, not an obligation to agree.
