## 0. Foundational Scope and Purpose

This protocol establishes a global method for discovering, mapping, characterizing, classifying, evaluating, and documenting **physical waste deposits that may contain valuable or recoverable materials**.

Its central idea is that a waste deposit should not automatically be treated as an undifferentiated mass. It may be a heterogeneous inventory containing metals, minerals, polymers, glass, reusable components, chemical residues, and materials whose composition is not yet known.

The protocol treats such deposits as **potential material mines**: physical accumulations whose contents can be investigated through evidence and whose suitable materials may be recovered, reused, or returned to productive material cycles when this can be done safely and with a defensible ecological outcome.

The protocol does not assume that every deposit contains valuable resources, that every identified substance can be separated, or that recovery is always desirable. Its purpose is to replace guesses with traceable observations, measurements, characterization, and verified outcomes.

### 0.1 Global and non-exclusive orientation

This is a global project protocol. It is not owned by, subordinate to, or defined by a particular country, government, company, commercial sector, or exclusive institution.

Its purpose is to preserve and expand shared knowledge about physical resources, material recovery, engineering possibilities, and ecological integrity. Its scope is not restricted to the economic interests of one nation or company, nor to the benefit of one human institution.

The protocol establishes its own technical and provenance framework. A specific site may have local conditions, access constraints, or operational requirements; those belong in that site's record and do not become the governing foundation of this global document.

### 0.2 Scope boundary — physical materials only

This document is specifically about **physical waste deposits and discarded material streams**.

It does not define discarded drafts, abandoned ideas, rejected text, software logs, or digital datasets as waste mines. Those subjects are outside this protocol's scope and must not be silently added to its taxonomy.

An earlier exploratory draft described three possible domains—cognitive, digital, and physical. The Architect subsequently clarified that the requested protocol concerns physical waste deposits and the valuable materials they may contain. The broader framing is retained here only as historical drafting context; it is not part of the adopted scope.

## 1. Core Concept: A Waste Deposit as a Material Mine

A **waste mine** is a mapped accumulation of discarded, residual, or stored physical materials that may contain substances or components suitable for reuse, repair, separation, recycling, or other verified recovery routes.

The word "mine" is a resource-mapping metaphor. It does not authorize uncontrolled excavation, indiscriminate sorting, or extraction without site assessment.

A waste mine may include:

1. Materials suitable for direct reuse.
2. Components suitable for repair, refurbishment, or disassembly.
3. Ferrous and non-ferrous metals.
4. Precious, rare-earth, and other critical materials where their presence is verified.
5. Plastics, polymers, rubber, and composite materials.
6. Glass, ceramics, concrete, bricks, and mineral aggregates.
7. Electronic and electrical equipment and its component materials.
8. Chemical and industrial residues that require controlled identification and specialist handling.
9. Organic and biological residues with appropriate material or biological recovery routes.
10. Unknown or mixed materials that must remain unclassified until adequately characterized.

The protocol distinguishes five separate questions:

- **Presence:** Is the material believed or demonstrated to exist?
- **Concentration and quantity:** How much is present, and in what proportion or form?
- **Technical recoverability:** Can a suitable process separate or recover it?
- **Practical recoverability:** Can that process work under the real conditions of this deposit?
- **Safe and ecologically defensible recoverability:** Can it be done without unacceptable harm to life, ecosystems, or surrounding systems?

These findings are not interchangeable. Detecting a metal does not prove that it is concentrated enough to recover, and a technically possible process is not automatically safe or practically justified.

## 2. Deposit Types and Material Categories

The mapping architecture must support different physical environments without assuming that all deposits have the same composition.

### 2.1 Physical deposit types

Examples include:

- Municipal solid-waste deposits and historical dumpsites.
- Industrial and manufacturing residues.
- Construction and demolition waste accumulations.
- Electrical and electronic waste (e-waste).
- Discarded vehicles, machinery, cables, and equipment.
- Battery and energy-system waste streams.
- Agricultural and food-processing residues.
- Abandoned or obsolete material stockpiles.
- Mixed deposits whose origin or history is only partly known.

The deposit type is a descriptive classification, not proof of its contents.

### 2.2 Ferrous and non-ferrous metals

Potential material classes include:

- Iron and steel.
- Copper and copper alloys.
- Aluminum and aluminum alloys.
- Zinc.
- Nickel.
- Tin.
- Lead.
- Cobalt.
- Chromium and manganese.
- Other metals identified through reliable characterization.

Records must distinguish the total mass of a metal-bearing object from the mass and concentration of the metal that could actually be recovered.

### 2.3 Precious, rare-earth, and critical materials

Potential examples include:

- Gold.
- Silver.
- Palladium and platinum-group metals.
- Rare-earth elements.
- Cobalt, nickel, lithium, and other materials considered critical in particular technical applications.
- Other scarce or functionally important elements when supported by evidence.

The labels "rare", "critical", or "precious" must not be assigned merely because a material is suspected to be present. The record must identify the evidence, material form, estimated concentration, and confidence level.

### 2.4 Plastics, polymers, and elastomers

Examples include:

- PET.
- HDPE.
- PVC.
- LDPE.
- PP.
- PS.
- Engineering plastics.
- Mixed, multilayer, filled, reinforced, or composite polymers.
- Rubber and elastomeric materials.

Identification should account for polymer type, additives, fillers, coatings, degradation, contamination, and mixed-material construction. Different polymers must not be treated as one interchangeable stream.

### 2.5 Glass, ceramics, and mineral materials

Examples include:

- Container glass.
- Flat glass.
- Technical glass.
- Ceramic materials.
- Concrete and mortar.
- Bricks and tiles.
- Mineral aggregates.
- Silicate-rich residues.

Color, composition, coatings, contamination, particle size, and previous use may determine whether a material can be reused or processed.

### 2.6 Electronic and composite materials

Examples include:

- Printed circuit boards.
- Cables and wiring.
- Motors and generators.
- Batteries and energy-storage components.
- Electronic components.
- Magnetic materials.
- Solar and electrical-system components.
- Composite assemblies containing metals, polymers, glass, ceramics, or other materials.

The complete assembly must be assessed. Recoverable components must not be separated from the safety context of the whole device or assembly.

### 2.7 Chemical and industrial residues

Examples may include:

- Oils and lubricants.
- Solvents.
- Acids and alkaline residues.
- Catalysts.
- Industrial process by-products.
- Battery electrolytes.
- Chemical-bearing sludges.
- Other stored or discarded chemical mixtures.

A chemical residue is not a recovery candidate merely because it may contain a useful substance. Its identity, concentration, stability, compatibility, contamination, and hazard profile must first be established by suitable analytical methods and qualified facilities.

### 2.8 Organic and biological residues

Examples include:

- Food and agricultural residues.
- Wood and plant matter.
- Fibrous materials.
- Organic industrial by-products.

Possible routes may include reuse, composting, biological conversion, or other controlled recovery methods, depending on composition, contamination, and local environmental conditions.

## 3. Waste Mine Mapping Architecture

Every mapped deposit must be represented as a structured site record. Each identified material stream must have its own linked material record.

### 3.1 Site identity

Each deposit receives a unique identifier:

`WASTE_MINE_ID`

The site record should contain:

- Unique site identifier.
- Deposit type.
- Geographic coordinates, when known and appropriate to record.
- Approximate surface area and depth.
- Deposit origin, if known.
- Estimated accumulation period.
- Current physical condition.
- Visible zones or layers.
- Date and method of survey.
- Evidence sources.
- Surveyor or responsible recording entity, where available.
- Confidence level.
- Access and information-sensitivity classification.

Unknown fields must remain explicitly unknown. Estimated boundaries must not be presented as measured boundaries.

### 3.2 Spatial mapping

Where the structure permits, divide a deposit into identifiable zones, cells, layers, or storage sections.

Possible mapping dimensions include:

- Surface and subsurface layers.
- Different accumulation periods.
- Material clusters.
- Storage or disposal sections.
- Areas with visibly different composition.
- Areas with instability, contamination, gas, fire, or other hazards.
- Areas requiring restricted access or specialist examination.

The map must differentiate between directly observed features, instrument-derived measurements, and inferred boundaries.

### 3.3 Material inventory

Every identified material stream receives a separate identifier:

`MATERIAL_RECORD_ID`

The material record should contain:

- Material name or provisional identity.
- Material category.
- Physical form and condition.
- Approximate location within the deposit.
- Estimated quantity and measured quantity, if available.
- Concentration or composition, where measured.
- Contamination status.
- Identification or test method.
- Evidence level and confidence.
- Possible reuse or recovery route.
- Known technical and safety limitations.
- Date and source of the record.

A visual estimate must never be represented as a laboratory result.

### 3.4 Spatial and information protection

A public knowledge map may show generalized site locations and material categories while withholding exact coordinates or sensitive operational details when disclosure could create safety, security, environmental, or exploitation risks.

The record should distinguish public information from restricted site information. Restriction must not be used to falsify, erase, or silently alter evidence.

## 4. Evidence, Sampling, and Material Characterization

A material classification must be supported by evidence appropriate to the claim being made.

### 4.1 Evidence levels

Use the following controlled labels:

- **OBSERVED** — visible or directly observed, without claiming laboratory identification.
- **MEASURED** — quantity or property measured using a stated method or instrument.
- **SAMPLED** — a sample was collected and its origin and handling were documented.
- **LAB_VERIFIED** — identity or composition supported by an appropriate laboratory analysis.
- **PROCESS_VERIFIED** — a controlled process has demonstrated a stated separation or recovery result.
- **ESTIMATED** — an estimate based on a disclosed method and assumptions.
- **INFERRED** — a reasoned conclusion derived from indirect evidence.
- **UNKNOWN** — available evidence is insufficient to support a reliable classification.

### 4.2 Sampling integrity

Sampling must be designed for the physical structure and possible hazards of the deposit.

A sampling record should state:

- Sampling location and depth, where applicable.
- Sampling method.
- Sample identifier.
- Date and responsible entity.
- Approximate sample mass or volume.
- Handling, storage, and transport conditions.
- Known limitations and possible sources of bias.
- Analytical method and laboratory reference, where applicable.

Samples must be representative enough for the claim being made. A single surface sample must not be used to characterize an entire heterogeneous deposit without justification.

### 4.3 Characterization requirements

Depending on the material and intended route, characterization may require determining:

- Chemical composition.
- Material concentration and distribution.
- Physical form and particle size.
- Moisture and degradation.
- Contamination and mixed-material content.
- Relevant mechanical or functional properties.
- Stability and compatibility.
- Hazard properties.
- Variability between zones or layers.

If a reliable identity or hazard profile cannot be established, the material remains uncharacterized.

## 5. Material Value and Recoverability Assessment

The protocol separates material value from the feasibility and desirability of recovering it.

### 5.1 Resource and functional value

Record potential usefulness based on:

- Direct reusability.
- Repair or refurbishment potential.
- Recyclability.
- Scarcity or technical criticality, where supported.
- Quality and condition.
- Potential substitution for newly extracted resources.
- Potential value as a component or secondary raw material.

Market price may be recorded as one time-dependent data point. It is not the sole measure of resource value and does not override safety or ecological constraints.

### 5.2 Technical recovery potential

Evaluate:

- Whether the material can be separated from the surrounding mixture.
- Whether a suitable process exists.
- Whether the deposit's composition allows separation.
- Whether contamination or degradation limits recovery.
- Whether the recovered output can meet a defined quality specification.
- Whether the process creates additional hazardous or unusable residues.

### 5.3 Practical recovery potential

Evaluate:

- Quantity and distribution of the material.
- Collection, access, and separation effort.
- Energy and water requirements.
- Transport and processing requirements.
- Expected yield and material losses.
- Required equipment and specialist capability.
- Output quality and availability of a real reuse route.
- Long-term maintenance and residual-management needs.

### 5.4 Ecological and safety assessment

Evaluate:

- Potential soil and water contamination.
- Air emissions, dust, and uncontrolled releases.
- Energy and water consumption.
- Transport and processing impacts.
- Residual material generated.
- Disturbance of habitats and ecological processes.
- Risks to people, animals, plants, and surrounding systems.
- Potential long-term harm from leaving the deposit undisturbed.

A high-value material must not automatically receive a high recovery priority. Recovery is not justified when the expected harm or uncontrolled risk exceeds the defensible benefit.

### 5.5 Separate assessment outcome

The record must report, separately:

1. Resource value.
2. Technical recovery potential.
3. Practical recovery potential.
4. Safety status.
5. Ecological outcome.
6. Overall decision and the evidence supporting it.

Do not collapse these dimensions into one unexplained score.

## 6. Deposit and Material Status Classification

Every site and material record must use an explicit status. Status changes require new evidence and a dated record.

- **UNSURVEYED** — no reliable site assessment exists.
- **MAPPED** — location and basic physical structure are recorded.
- **PRELIMINARILY_CLASSIFIED** — visible material groups are recorded without claiming unverified composition.
- **SAMPLED** — appropriate samples have been collected and documented.
- **CHARACTERIZED** — composition and relevant properties have been assessed.
- **RECOVERY_CANDIDATE** — a plausible recovery or reuse route has been identified.
- **RECOVERY_VERIFIED** — a controlled process has demonstrated the stated recovery outcome.
- **REUSE_READY** — the material has been verified as suitable for a defined reuse route.
- **CONTROLLED_HAZARD** — controlled handling or specialist assessment is required.
- **UNCHARACTERIZED** — identity, composition, or hazard remains uncertain.
- **ECOLOGICALLY_UNSUITABLE** — recovery is not justified under the assessed conditions.
- **STABILIZATION_REQUIRED** — the priority is to prevent release or movement of hazardous material.
- **CLOSED_WITH_EVIDENCE** — assessment is complete and the reason for closure is recorded.

A status is a description of the evidence and decision state. It is not a guarantee that a deposit is safe or economically recoverable.

## 7. Safety and Ecological Integrity Gate

Protection of life and ecological systems is a non-negotiable condition of mapping and recovery.

The following controls apply:

1. Unknown substances must not be mixed, heated, burned, crushed, or chemically processed to discover their contents.
2. Batteries, pressurized containers, reactive substances, toxic residues, unstable stockpiles, and suspected contaminated zones require specialist assessment.
3. Chemical identification and recovery must use appropriate analytical methods, controlled facilities, and qualified personnel.
4. Open burning and uncontrolled chemical or metallurgical extraction are not accepted recovery methods.
5. Sampling must avoid unnecessary exposure, dust generation, fire risk, and environmental release.
6. A recovery operation must stop when unexpected hazards, uncontrolled emissions, instability, or unanticipated reactions appear.
7. Residual materials, by-products, and treatment outputs must be documented; they must not disappear from the inventory.
8. Materials of unknown composition must not be assigned to ordinary recycling streams without adequate characterization.
9. If the available evidence cannot demonstrate acceptable safety, the material must not be processed through that route.
10. Site stabilization, isolation, monitoring, or non-intervention may be preferable to recovery.

This protocol is a mapping, evidence, classification, and decision framework. It does not provide improvised chemical extraction recipes or instructions for uncontrolled processing.

## 8. Resource Recovery and Management Hierarchy

Select a route according to material condition, evidence, safety, and ecological outcome.

Preferred routes, when suitable, are:

1. Prevent unnecessary disposal and preserve usable materials before they become mixed.
2. Direct reuse.
3. Repair and refurbishment.
4. Component recovery and disassembly.
5. Sorting and material separation.
6. Mechanical recycling or other established material-recovery routes.
7. Controlled chemical or metallurgical recovery in qualified facilities.
8. Other verified recovery routes suited to the material.
9. Stabilization, isolation, or monitored containment when recovery is unsuitable.
10. Secure management of residual materials that cannot be safely reused or recovered.

This is a decision hierarchy, not a claim that one route is suitable for every material. Leaving a deposit undisturbed or stabilizing it may be the best outcome where intervention would cause greater harm.

## 9. Material Flow and Mass Balance

Every controlled recovery assessment must record material movement through the process.

The basic material-flow model is:

`INPUT MATERIAL → SEPARATION / TREATMENT → RECOVERED OUTPUTS + RESIDUAL OUTPUTS + LOSSES`

At minimum, record:

- Input mass by source stream.
- Recovered mass by material type.
- Residual mass by output category.
- Process losses.
- Unaccounted mass.
- Measurement uncertainty.
- Water or other significant process inputs where relevant.
- Sampling and measurement method.

The mass balance must be checked for consistency. Unexplained differences must remain visible and investigated; they must not be silently adjusted to make the totals agree.

A reported recovery yield must identify its denominator and basis. For example, a yield calculated from the mass of a selected component is not the same as a yield calculated from the total mixed feed.

## 10. Global Knowledge Map and Record Interoperability

The protocol supports a global knowledge map connecting independently surveyed physical deposits without requiring ownership or control by a single nation or company.

The map may contain:

- Waste mine site records.
- Spatial zones and deposit layers.
- Material inventories.
- Characterization and sampling records.
- Evidence and confidence metadata.
- Recovery and reuse routes.
- Verified process outcomes and yields.
- Environmental observations and constraints.
- Residual and stabilization records.
- Failed attempts and unresolved questions.
- Reusable engineering designs and lessons.

Records should use stable identifiers, explicit units, dates, clear evidence labels, and documented uncertainty so they can be compared across sites.

### 10.1 Data integrity

Every material claim must retain its source and evidence trail. Corrections must preserve the earlier value, the corrected value, the reason, the date, and the responsible source.

Estimated, inferred, and verified values must remain distinguishable in every export or summary.

### 10.2 Public and restricted information

The knowledge map should maximize useful public knowledge while protecting exact site details when disclosure could create material risks. Public summaries may generalize location or omit sensitive details; internal evidence records should retain the information necessary for audit by authorized reviewers.

### 10.3 No exclusive ownership assumption

The protocol does not assign exclusive ownership of global knowledge or of a material category to any one institution. Individual site records may describe their documented origin, access conditions, and custodial context without changing the global and non-exclusive identity of the protocol.

## 11. Operational Implementation Stages

### Stage A — Discovery

Identify a potential physical waste deposit and collect available historical, spatial, and observational information.

### Stage B — Preliminary mapping

Assign a site identifier, map visible zones, record deposit type, and identify obvious restrictions or hazards. Do not claim unverified chemical composition.

### Stage C — Initial material inventory

Create separate records for visible material groups and unknown mixtures. Record estimated quantities only with a stated basis and confidence level.

### Stage D — Safe characterization

Determine the sampling and analytical work needed. Use appropriate specialists and facilities for hazardous, reactive, or unknown materials.

### Stage E — Resource assessment

Evaluate resource value, technical recoverability, practical feasibility, safety, and ecological cost as separate dimensions.

### Stage F — Route selection

Select a reuse, repair, separation, recycling, controlled recovery, stabilization, or non-intervention route based on evidence and the safety gate.

### Stage G — Controlled verification

Where processing is justified, verify input, recovered output, residuals, losses, quality, and environmental observations under controlled conditions.

### Stage H — Knowledge integration

Update the global map with verified results, failures, uncertainties, corrections, and reusable engineering knowledge.

## 12. Sovereign Engineering Application

This protocol applies the independent engineering approach recorded in `SOVEREIGN_ENGINEERING_PRINCIPLE.md`.

The practical sequence is:

- Understand the deposit and its materials before selecting equipment.
- Define the required function before purchasing or designing a machine.
- Evaluate available resources and reusable components.
- Select modular separation and measurement systems where justified.
- Prefer repairable, adaptable, and maintainable equipment.
- Verify process performance through measured results.
- Avoid machinery or processing routes whose energy, water, safety, or ecological costs exceed their defensible benefit.
- Use specialist facilities when the process exceeds the available technical or safety capability.

The material determines the process requirements. A machine must not be imposed on a deposit merely because it is available.

Sovereign engineering does not mean rejecting external tools or expertise. It means understanding the problem, independently evaluating available options, and selecting the safest and most suitable solution rather than treating a ready-made system as the only possible answer.
